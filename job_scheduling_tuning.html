Fairshare and Scheduler Tuning: LSF, Slurm, and Grid Engine

How each scheduler computes priority, what to tune when job delays start creeping up, and how to turn observability into safe tuning.

1. LSF: fairshare dynamic priority

LSF calculates a dynamic priority for each user or group at each level of the share tree. The scheduler dispatches from the highest-priority node first.

                              number_shares
dynamic_priority = ─────────────────────────────────────────────────
                     cpu_time            × CPU_TIME_FACTOR
                   + run_time            × RUN_TIME_FACTOR
                   + (1 + job_slots)     × RUN_JOB_FACTOR
                   + committed_run_time  × COMMITTED_RUN_TIME_FACTOR
                   + hist_run_time       × HIST_RUN_TIME_FACTOR   (if ENABLE_HIST_RUN_TIME=Y)
                   + fairshare_adj       × FAIRSHARE_ADJUSTMENT_FACTOR

Newer releases add GPU terms, such as GPU_RUN_TIME_FACTOR, to the denominator.

The core idea

  • Numerator: the shares you were given. This is your entitlement.
  • Denominator: what you have consumed recently. This is your usage.
  • Result: more usage means lower priority; more shares means higher priority.

What each term means

  • number_shares: shares assigned in USER_SHARES or FAIRSHARE in lsb.users or lsb.queues.
  • cpu_time: cumulative CPU time, decayed over time.
  • run_time: wall-clock time of running jobs, decayed over time.
  • job_slots: slots the user is currently using. The "+1" keeps the denominator from being zero.
  • committed_run_time: the run limit committed by running jobs. This penalizes users who reserve long runtimes up front.
  • fairshare_adj: a value from a custom plugin (libfairshareadjust).

Decay with HIST_HOURS: HIST_HOURS (default 5) controls how fast past usage fades. After HIST_HOURS, one hour of usage counts as only 0.1 hours. Usage decays exponentially, so a user is gradually forgiven for past use.

Default factors: CPU_TIME_FACTOR=0.7, RUN_TIME_FACTOR=0.7, RUN_JOB_FACTOR=3.0, COMMITTED_RUN_TIME_FACTOR=0.

"Delay is creeping": what to tweak

There are two ways to read that symptom.

A. Jobs wait longer and longer (priority starvation)

This is a fairshare-math problem: the denominator keeps growing.

  • Lower HIST_HOURS. Past usage decays faster, so heavy users regain priority sooner.
  • Lower RUN_JOB_FACTOR. Reduces the penalty for many concurrent jobs. It dominates for high-throughput users with many small jobs.
  • Lower RUN_TIME_FACTOR or CPU_TIME_FACTOR if long jobs are dragging priority down.
  • Set COMMITTED_RUN_TIME_FACTOR to 0 (or near it) if users set big -W limits and are punished for them up front.
  • Rebalance number_shares. This raises the numerator directly.

Diagnose: bhpart -r or bqueues -r -l show shares, priority, and usage per tree node, so you can see which term is driving priority down.

B. The scheduler itself is slow (dispatch latency, mbatchd load)

Fairshare adds scheduling cost, so tune it as overhead:

  • Remove or simplify the fairshare_adj plugin. It runs every scheduling session, so a slow plugin hurts every cycle.
  • Flatten deep share trees, or avoid [default, N] in large groups. That entry creates a share node per user, so thousands of users means thousands of nodes recalculated each cycle.
  • Disable ENABLE_HIST_RUN_TIME if you don't need it.

Non-fairshare knobs in lsb.params:

  • JOB_SCHEDULING_INTERVAL, MBD_SLEEP_TIME: scheduling cadence.
  • MAX_CONCURRENT_QUERY, MBD_QUERY_CPUS, MBD_REFRESH_TIME: keep heavy bjobs polling off the submit path.
  • NEWJOB_REFRESH=Y: new submissions reach the scheduler faster.
  • LSB_MAX_PACK_JOBS (bsub -pack) and job arrays: cut per-job submission overhead.
  • CONDENSE_PENDING_REASONS: less pending-reason bookkeeping.

Diagnosing scheduler latency

  • badmin perfmon start / view: submission rate, scheduling time, and query load.
  • badmin diagnose: more scheduler detail.
  • badmin schddebug / LSB_TIME_MBSCHD: time per scheduling phase, which tells you whether fairshare is actually the bottleneck.
In short: first decide whether it's priority starvation or scheduler latency. For starvation, tune HIST_HOURS and RUN_JOB_FACTOR, since those usually dominate. For latency, look at share-tree size, the adjustment plugin, and query load on mbatchd, and confirm with perfmon data.

2. Slurm: multifactor priority

Slurm uses a different model. Fairshare is one weighted term inside a bigger multifactor sum, not the whole formula as in LSF.

Job_priority = site_factor
             + PriorityWeightAge       × age_factor
             + PriorityWeightAssoc     × assoc_factor
             + PriorityWeightFairshare × fairshare_factor
             + PriorityWeightJobSize   × job_size_factor
             + PriorityWeightPartition × partition_factor
             + PriorityWeightQOS       × qos_factor
             + Σ (PriorityWeightTRES_i × TRES_factor_i)
             − nice_factor

Every factor is normalized to 0.0–1.0. The weights (integers in slurm.conf) decide how much each factor matters. The key point: fairshare only matters as much as PriorityWeightFairshare allows relative to the other weights.

The fairshare factor itself

Classic algorithm

F = 2^( −U_effective / S_normalized / PriorityDampeningFactor )
  • S: your shares ÷ total shares at that level, multiplied down the tree.
  • U: your decayed usage ÷ total usage.
  • When U = S (you used exactly your share), F = 0.5. Unused share pushes F toward 1; overuse pushes it toward 0.
  • Effective usage also pulls in the parent account's usage:
U_eff = U_self + (U_parent_eff − U_self) × (S_self / S_siblings)

Fair Tree (default since 19.05)

  • At each level of the account tree it computes LevelFS = S / U, where S and U are your fraction of shares and usage among your siblings.
  • It walks the tree depth-first, sorting siblings by LevelFS. Accounts that deserve more priority are visited first, and all their users rank above the next account's users.
  • Final factor: fairshare_factor = rank / total_users.
  • Practical effect: one heavy user drags their whole account down, but cannot hurt users in other accounts.

Usage decay

usage(t) = usage(0) × 0.5^( t / PriorityDecayHalfLife )
  • PriorityDecayHalfLife defaults to 7 days. It is the Slurm equivalent of LSF's HIST_HOURS.
  • Usage is charged in TRES-billing units, weighted by TRESBillingWeights per partition.
  • PriorityCalcPeriod (default 5 minutes) sets how often priorities are recalculated.

"Delay is creeping": what to tweak

A. Some users' jobs keep sitting longer (priority problem)

  • Lower PriorityDecayHalfLife. Past usage is forgiven faster. Seven days is long for bursty research groups.
  • Rebalance weights. Raise PriorityWeightAge and set PriorityMaxAge so long-waiting jobs eventually rise regardless of fairshare, or lower PriorityWeightFairshare if it dominates.
  • Adjust shares with sacctmgr modify account/user ... set fairshare=N.
  • Check TRESBillingWeights. GPU or memory-heavy jobs may be charged more than you intend.
  • Last resort: PriorityUsageResetPeriod resets usage periodically, or sacctmgr modify user ... set rawusage=0 resets one user.

Diagnose: sprio -l shows which factor dominates a pending job; sshare -l -a shows shares, usage, and the fairshare factor across the tree.

B. The controller is slow (scheduler or RPC latency)

In SchedulerParameters:

  • default_queue_depth, sched_interval, sched_min_interval: how much of the queue the main scheduler examines each pass, and how often.
  • bf_interval, bf_max_job_test, bf_max_job_user, bf_window, bf_resolution, bf_continue, bf_yield_interval: backfill is usually the most expensive pass. A large bf_window with a small bf_resolution is a classic slowdown.
  • max_rpc_cnt: the scheduler defers when the RPC backlog is high, protecting submit responsiveness.
  • defer, batch_sched_delay: stop scheduling on every submit, which helps high submit rates.

Other areas:

  • Raise PriorityCalcPeriod if fairshare recalculation is costly on a large association tree.
  • Check slurmdbd and MariaDB. A slow accounting backend, missing purge settings (PurgeJobAfter and similar), or a huge job table can back up slurmctld.
  • Reduce user polling. Heavy squeue loops from scripts or workflow engines hammer the controller. Use job arrays, and rl_* rate limiting on newer versions.

Diagnose: sdiag shows main and backfill cycle times, queue depth tested, RPC counts by type and user, and agent queue size. It is the counterpart of LSF's badmin perfmon.

In short: in Slurm, fairshare is one weighted factor, so run sprio first to see whether fairshare is actually what's holding jobs back. Then tune PriorityDecayHalfLife and the weight balance, especially age versus fairshare. If it's controller latency instead, use sdiag to see whether backfill cycle time or RPC load is the problem, tune bf_* and max_rpc_cnt, and check slurmdbd health.

LSF vs Slurm in one line: LSF computes priority as shares divided by weighted usage at each tree level. Slurm turns shares and usage into a normalized 0–1 factor and adds it to other weighted factors.

3. Grid Engine: tickets and urgency

Grid Engine (SGE, then Univa, now Altair) is the most layered of the three. Fairshare lives inside a ticket system, and tickets are only one of three terms in the final priority.

prio = weight_priority × npprior
     + weight_urgency  × nurg
     + weight_ticket   × ntckts

Weights are set with qconf -msconf. Defaults: weight_priority=1.0, weight_urgency=0.1, weight_ticket=0.01.

TermWhat it is
nppriorNormalized POSIX priority from qsub -p (−1023 to 1024, mapped to 0–1)
nurgNormalized urgency
ntcktsNormalized ticket count. This is where fairshare lives.

Urgency

urgency = rrcontr + wtcontr + dlcontr
  • rrcontr: resource requests × the urgency value of each complex (qconf -mc), multiplied by slots.
  • wtcontr: waiting_time × weight_waiting_time. This is the age factor.
  • dlcontr: weight_deadline / time_left_to_deadline.

Tickets: the fairshare part

tickets = share_tree_tickets + functional_tickets + override_tickets

Pool sizes are set by weight_tickets_share, weight_tickets_functional, and override tickets assigned directly. policy_hierarchy (default OFS) sets the order in which policies apply.

Share tree policy (usage-based, historical)

  • Long-term entitlement: each node's share ÷ its siblings' total shares, multiplied down the tree. Same idea as Slurm's normalized shares.
  • Actual usage: usage = cpu × w_cpu + mem × w_mem + io × w_io, with weights from usage_weight_list (default: CPU only).
  • Decay: usage halves every halftime hours (default 168, i.e. 7 days), the counterpart of LSF's HIST_HOURS and Slurm's PriorityDecayHalfLife.
  • Short-term entitlement: long-term entitlement adjusted up or down by how far usage is below or above target. Underusers get more tickets; overusers get fewer.
  • compensation_factor: caps that adjustment. With 5, an idle node can't get more than 5× its long-term share, so one returning user can't flood the cluster.

Functional policy (current, no history)

  • Splits weight_tickets_functional by functional shares across user, project, department, and job.
  • The split is controlled by weight_user, weight_project, weight_department, and weight_job.
  • No memory of past usage; it only reflects what is running and pending now.

Override

Tickets given manually to a user, project, or job for a temporary boost.

One way to frame it: share tree is "fair over the past week," functional is "fair right now," and override is "admin says so."

"Delay is creeping": what to tweak

A. Users' or projects' jobs keep waiting longer (priority problem)

  • Lower halftime. Past usage is forgiven faster.
  • Lower compensation_factor if one returning heavy user swings priority too hard.
  • Shift ticket pools. Raise weight_tickets_functional relative to weight_tickets_share for less history-driven behavior.
  • Raise weight_waiting_time or weight_urgency so aging jobs eventually win.
  • Watch weight_ticket. It's tiny by default (0.01). If you've raised it, tickets may now drown out everything else.
  • Adjust node shares with qconf -mstree.

Diagnose: qstat -pri (priority breakdown), qstat -urg (urgency), qstat -ext (tickets), sge_share_mon (share tree usage vs. entitlement, live).

B. qmaster or scheduler is slow (latency problem)

  • report_pjob_tickets=false: computing tickets for every pending job is expensive with large queues. A classic fix.
  • max_functional_jobs_to_schedule: limits how many pending jobs get functional tickets each pass.
  • max_pending_tasks_per_job: keeps large array jobs from blowing up scheduling work.
  • reprioritize_interval=0: disables dynamic renicing of running jobs, cutting execd/qmaster chatter.
  • schedule_interval, flush_submit_sec, flush_finish_sec: flush values set too low make the scheduler run on every submit.
  • max_reservation: resource reservation (-R y) is expensive. Keep it small.
  • params PROFILE=1 (and MONITOR=1): logs time per scheduling phase, showing whether ticket calculation is the bottleneck.

qmaster side: set worker, listener, and (Univa/Altair) reader threads so qstat floods stay off the main path. Classic spooling, or BDB on slow or NFS storage, is a common hidden cause of submit latency. qping -info <qmaster> <port> qmaster 1 shows thread status and message backlog.

In short: Grid Engine priority is POSIX priority plus urgency plus tickets, and fairshare is the share-tree portion of the tickets. For starvation, use qstat -pri to see which term dominates, then tune halftime, compensation_factor, and the share/functional ticket balance. For latency, the big wins are report_pjob_tickets=false, limiting functional ticket calculation, and fixing flush intervals and spooling.

5. Which handles high-rate short jobs best?

For high-rate short jobs, LSF's formula is the best balanced of the three. The reason is more interesting than "LSF is fastest," and there are caveats.

Why short jobs are a hard case

Each short job is too brief to build up much CPU or run time, so a formula based purely on historical usage barely notices a user who floods the queue with 100,000 thirty-second jobs. A good formula for this workload needs two kinds of terms:

  1. An instantaneous term that reacts within the current scheduling cycle to how much someone is running right now.
  2. A decayed historical term so usage still counts once the burst ends.

How each one handles it

LSF: best balanced for this workload

  • The denominator already mixes both kinds: instantaneous (1 + job_slots) × RUN_JOB_FACTOR, and historical cpu_time and run_time, decayed by HIST_HOURS.
  • Fast reaction: job_slots changes the moment jobs dispatch, so a user flooding short jobs loses priority within the same scheduling session.
  • One knob for burstiness: RUN_JOB_FACTOR versus RUN_TIME_FACTOR sets how much you care about concurrency versus duration.
  • Built-in throughput tools: job packs, chunk jobs, and arrays. Part of why EDA, heavy on short regression and characterization jobs, has traditionally been LSF territory.

Grid Engine: most expressive, most expensive to compute

  • It can reach the same balance: the functional policy is the "right now" term, the share tree is the historical term, and compensation_factor caps overcorrection, which LSF doesn't have. On paper, arguably the best design.
  • The cost: tickets are calculated per pending job, which becomes the bottleneck at high submit rates. That's why report_pjob_tickets=false and max_functional_jobs_to_schedule exist.
  • The tradeoff: scheduler throughput for policy richness.

Slurm: most principled and transparent, but lags on bursts

  • No instantaneous term: the fairshare factor is purely decayed history, recalculated every PriorityCalcPeriod (default 5 minutes). Nothing like LSF's job_slots.
  • Result: a burst of short jobs can take over the queue before fairshare catches up.
  • Strengths: Fair Tree's account isolation is excellent, and normalized 0–1 factors make tuning predictable.
  • How sites compensate: QOS and association limits (MaxJobs, MaxSubmitJobs, GrpTRES). That works, but it's a separate mechanism, not a balanced formula.
  • Per-job overhead: controller and slurmdbd cost per job is higher; Slurm has a dedicated high-throughput tuning guide for this reason.

Summary

Reacts to bursts instantlyRemembers historyCost at high rateVerdict for short jobs
LSFYes (RUN_JOB_FACTOR)YesLowBest balanced
Grid EngineYes (functional)Yes (share tree)High (per-job tickets)Richest, but costly
SlurmNo (needs QOS limits)YesMedium–highCleanest math, laggy for bursts
In short: LSF balances best because its denominator combines an instantaneous concurrency term, RUN_JOB_FACTOR × slots, with decayed historical usage. Fairshare corrects within a scheduling cycle and still remembers the burst afterward. Grid Engine can express the same thing with functional plus share-tree tickets, but ticket calculation gets expensive at high rates. Slurm's fairshare is purely historical, so for short bursts you lean on QOS limits instead.
Caveat: a good formula doesn't fix scheduler throughput. If submission latency is the actual problem, job packs, arrays, and the latency knobs above matter more than the fairshare math.

6. From observability to tuning: do shops auto-tune fairshare?

Mostly no, at least not fully automatically. Most LSF shops run a human-in-the-loop process: dashboards and alerts flag a fairness problem, an admin proposes a change, it gets approved, and someone runs badmin reconfig. Closed-loop auto-tuning of RUN_JOB_FACTOR or HIST_HOURS is rare.

The reason: fairshare is a policy that encodes agreements about who is entitled to what. It is not a performance metric. An auto-tuner needs an objective function, and "fair" is partly political. What shops do automate is usually share values fed from budgets or project allocations, plus guardrails.

In practice, "semi-automatic" is a five-step pipeline.

Step 1. Turn observability into fairness SLOs

  • Entitlement error per group: actual_usage_fraction − entitled_share_fraction over a rolling window (24 hours or 7 days).
  • Pending time p50/p95 per group and queue. Raw averages hide starvation.
  • Max pending age. The direct starvation signal.
  • Recovery time: after a group's burst ends, how long until its priority returns to normal. This directly measures whether HIST_HOURS is right.
  • Scheduler health: mbschd cycle time, dispatch latency, and submit rate. These separate a fairness problem from a throughput problem.

Step 2. Collect the data

SourceGives you
bhpart -r, bqueues -r -lShares, dynamic priority, and usage per tree node
bjobs -u all -pPending jobs and pending reasons
lsb.acct / lsb.streamPer-job history and real-time job events
badmin perfmonSubmit rate, scheduling time, query load
IBM RTM or LSF ExplorerPackaged versions of the above, if licensed

A small exporter that scrapes these into Prometheus works well. If you already run Prometheus and Grafana for the cluster, this is mostly new exporters and panels, not a new stack.

Step 3. Map symptoms to parameters

This is the "model." In most shops it's a rules table, not machine learning:

SymptomLikely causeAdjust
High-concurrency groups dominate during burstsConcurrency penalty too weakRaise RUN_JOB_FACTOR
A group stays starved long after its burst endsUsage forgiven too slowlyLower HIST_HOURS
Long-runner groups always rank lowRun time weighted too heavilyLower RUN_TIME_FACTOR
Usage diverges from entitlement over a weekShares don't match realityRebalance USER_SHARES
Priority looks fine but dispatch is slowNot a fairshare problemScheduler and mbatchd tuning

Step 4. Run a cheap what-if before changing anything

The dynamic priority formula is just arithmetic. Replay a day of lsb.acct data, recompute each group's priority with candidate factor values, and see how the ranking would have shifted. It's an approximation, since it ignores the feedback effect of the changed dispatch order, but it catches bad ideas before they reach production. IBM also offers an LSF simulator for full trace replay, if your license includes it.

Step 5. Make the change with guardrails

  • One parameter at a time, in bounded steps, e.g. no more than ±25% change to RUN_JOB_FACTOR at once.
  • Wait between changes, at least one or two HIST_HOURS periods, or effects are still settling when you judge them.
  • Track every change with a rollback. Keep lsb.params and lsb.users in git, and log each badmin reconfig alongside dashboard annotations.
  • A human approves. The tool proposes; a person decides.

Where real automation does fit

LSF has a built-in hook: the FAIRSHARE_ADJUSTMENT_FACTOR plugin (libfairshareadjust). It adds a per-user term computed by your code every scheduling cycle. A common use is correcting for longer-term entitlement error than HIST_HOURS can see, such as giving a small boost to groups under their weekly entitlement. Keep it cheap and cached: it runs every cycle, and a slow plugin is exactly the scheduler latency problem from section 1.

In short: define fairness SLOs (entitlement error, p95 pending time, recovery time after a burst) and export LSF data into Prometheus. Map each symptom to a specific factor. What-if test changes against accounting replay, and make them one at a time with a human approving. Automation belongs in share values or a lightweight fairshare-adjust plugin, not in rewriting the factors on the fly.
Defaults and parameter names vary by version. Check your scheduler's documentation before changing production settings.
